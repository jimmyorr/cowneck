## Sources by claim

### The bath, the golden wreath and Galileo's balance
- **Vitruvius's account of the bath and votive wreath.** Vitruvius, *De architectura*, Book IX, Preface 9–12 (written 1st century BC, roughly two centuries after Archimedes' death in 212 BC). Hiero II supplied pure gold for a votive wreath for a temple, suspected silver adulteration, and asked Archimedes to investigate. Archimedes stepped into a bath, noticed water overflowing as he sank in, ran home naked crying *heurēka* ("I have found it"), and compared the water displaced by equal weights of gold and the wreath. Sources: Wikipedia ("Archimedes", "Hiero II of Syracuse"); Chris Rorres, *The Golden Crown (Sources)*, NYU (`math.nyu.edu/Archimedes/Crown/Vitruvius.html`).
- **Galileo's critique (1586) and the hydrostatic balance.** Galileo Galilei, *La Bilancetta* ("The Little Balance", 1586; written when Galileo was 22). Numerical check on overflow volume: gold density ≈ 19.32 g/cm³, silver ≈ 10.49 g/cm³. For a 2 lb (~907 g) crown with 25% (~227 g) silver substituted for gold, pure gold volume is 907 / 19.32 ≈ 46.95 cm³, while the adulterated crown volume is 680 / 19.32 + 227 / 10.49 ≈ 35.20 + 21.64 = 56.84 cm³, an increase of ~9.9 cm³ (roughly two teaspoons, ~10 mL). Across a 20 cm circular basin (area ≈ 314 cm²), 10 cm³ raises the water level by ~0.32 mm ("less than half a millimeter"). Sources: Rice University Galileo Project (`galileo.library.rice.edu/sci/instruments/balance.html`); Chris Rorres, NYU Archimedes Crown analysis; Wikipedia ("Archimedes").
- **Archimedes' principle.** *On Floating Bodies*, Book I, Proposition 7 (upthrust equals weight of displaced fluid). Source: Wikipedia ("Archimedes").

### Life, family, Alexandria, the *On Spirals* trap and levers
- **Lost biography by Heracleides.** Cited by Eutocius of Ascalon (6th century AD). Sources: MacTutor ("Archimedes"); Wikipedia ("Archimedes").
- **Birth c. 287 BC and age 75.** Derived from the 12th-century Byzantine writer John Tzetzes (*Chiliades*, Book II), who states Archimedes lived 75 years, counted backward from his death in 212 BC. Hedged as resting on an unreliable 12th-century Byzantine source. Sources: MacTutor; Wikipedia.
- **Father Phidias.** Archimedes, *The Sand Reckoner* (*Psammites*): "Eudoxus declared it to be about nine times as great, and Pheidias my father twelve times..." Source: T. L. Heath, *The Works of Archimedes* (*The Sand-Reckoner*).
- **Relation to Hiero II vs. modest origin.** Plutarch, *Life of Marcellus* 14.7 ("whose friend and near relation he was"); Cicero (*Tusculan Disputations* V.64, *humilem homunculum*) and Silius Italicus suggest humble social standing. Sources: MacTutor; Wikipedia ("Archimedes").
- **Dragged to the baths, drawing in ashes and olive oil; Plutarch's claim that he despised engineering.** Plutarch, *Life of Marcellus* 17.3–6: servants hauling him to the baths to wash and anoint him, tracing geometric figures in the ashes of the stove/brazier and drawing lines with his fingernail in the oil on his body; repudiating engineering as "sordid and ignoble." Counter-evidence for his lost mechanical treatise *On Sphere-Making*: Pappus of Alexandria (*Collection*, Book VIII). Sources: MacTutor; Wikipedia ("Archimedes").
- **Alexandrian correspondents and the two false theorems.** Treatises addressed to Conon of Samos (c. 280–220 BC), Dositheus of Pelusium, and Eratosthenes of Cyrene. Verbatim quotation from the preface to *On Spirals* (Heath translation, quoted in MacTutor): "...so that those who claim to discover everything, but produce no proofs of the same, may be confuted as having pretended to discover the impossible." Sources: MacTutor ("Archimedes"); Heath, *The Works of Archimedes*.
- **Law of the lever, Pappus quotation, moving the ship, and Archimedes' screw.** *On the Equilibrium of Planes*, Book I, Propositions 6–7 (weights balance at distances inversely proportional to their weights). "Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth": Pappus of Alexandria, *Collection*, Book VIII (4th century AD). Moving a three-masted merchantman loaded with cargo and crew single-handed via a compound pulley: Plutarch, *Life of Marcellus* 14.8. Earlier Egyptian and Mesopotamian/Assyrian origins of the screw pump: hedged with "versions of it had probably irrigated Egypt and Mesopotamia before he was born." Sources: MacTutor; Wikipedia ("Archimedes", "Archimedes' screw").

### Central mathematical idea: exhaustion, *Measurement of a Circle*, and sphere/cylinder
- **Method of exhaustion and Eudoxus.** Originated with Eudoxus of Cnidus (4th century BC; Euclid, *Elements* Book XII); double *reductio ad absurdum* squeezing curved magnitudes between inscribed and circumscribed polygons. Sources: Wikipedia ("Archimedes", "Method of exhaustion"); Encyclopedia.com (DSB).
- ***Measurement of a Circle* (Proposition 3).** Starts with regular inscribed hexagon (side $1/2$ for diameter $1$, perimeter $3$) and circumscribed hexagon (perimeter $2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.464$); doubles sides four times ($6 \to 12 \to 24 \to 48 \to 96$); uses fractional bounds $265/153 < \sqrt{3} < 1351/780$ without explaining their derivation; proves $3\frac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\frac{1}{7}$, i.e., $223/71 < \pi < 22/7$ ($3.140845... < 3.141592... < 3.142857...$). Sources: Heath (*Measurement of a Circle*); MacTutor; G. M. Phillips, *American Mathematical Monthly* 88(3) (1981).
- ***Quadrature of the Parabola* and *On the Sphere and Cylinder*.** Sphere inscribed in a cylinder of equal height and diameter has $2/3$ of the cylinder's volume and $2/3$ of its total surface area (including bases). Sources: MacTutor; Wikipedia ("On the Sphere and Cylinder").

### Siege of Syracuse, burning mirrors, death, and Cicero at the tomb
- **Hiero's death (215 BC) and Roman siege under Marcellus (214–212 BC).** Polybius (*Histories* VIII.3–7), Livy (*Ab Urbe Condita* XXIV.34), Plutarch (*Marcellus* 14–17): long-range catapults, short-range scorpions firing through narrow slots, and the iron claw (*manus ferrea*) lifting ships by the prow and dropping them. Roman soldiers bolting at a bit of rope or timber over the wall, and Marcellus's "geometrical Briareus": Plutarch, *Marcellus* 17.1–3 (Loeb translation).
- **Burning mirrors.** Absent from Polybius, Livy and Plutarch; Polybius and Plutarch describe night naval attacks. First clear description of burning mirrors is Anthemius of Tralles (*On Burning-Glasses*, c. AD 500, ~700 years later). Modern tests: Ioannis Sakkas near Athens in 1973 (70 sailors with copper-coated mirrors, tarred rowboat smoking at ~50 m); MIT class 2.009 in 2005 (stationary wooden hull mockup, followed by San Francisco Bay trial). Sources: Chris Rorres, *Refuting the Legend*, NYU (`math.nyu.edu/Archimedes/Mirrors/legend/legend.html`); Wikipedia ("Archimedes", "Archimedes' heat ray").
- **Fall of Syracuse (212 BC), festival of Artemis, three death stories, and "Do not disturb my circles."** Plutarch (*Marcellus* 19.4–6) gives all three death accounts and Marcellus shunning the killer. "Do not disturb my circles" (*Noli turbare circulos meos*) is not in Livy or Plutarch; Valerius Maximus (*Facta et dicta memorabilia* VIII.7.ext.7, 1st century AD) writes *noli, obsecro, istum disturbare* ("I beg of you, do not disturb this"). Sources: MacTutor; Wikipedia ("Archimedes"); Loeb *Marcellus*.
- **Marcellus's bronze planetarium and Cicero finding the tomb (75 BC).** Cicero, *De re publica* I.21–22 (geared bronze sphere kept by Marcellus). Antikythera shipwreck recovery (1901). Cicero serving as quaestor in Sicily in 75 BC finding the overgrown tomb with sphere and cylinder outside the Agrigentine Gate (and boasting as "a man from Arpinum"): *Tusculan Disputations* V.64–66. Sources: Chris Rorres, NYU (`math.nyu.edu/Archimedes/Tomb/Cicero.html`); MacTutor; Wikipedia.

### The Archimedes Palimpsest (and 2026 Blois leaf)
- **Constantinople copy (~AD 950) and Ioannes Myronas (April 1229).** Seven Archimedes treatises copied in iron-gall ink in Constantinople c. 950; taken to Jerusalem after the 1204 Fourth Crusade sack; unbound along with six other manuscripts, scraped/washed, cut in half, rotated 90 degrees, and overwritten with church prayers by the priest Ioannes Myronas, dated April 1229. Preserved at Mar Saba and later back in Constantinople; William Noel's observation that recycling the codex as a prayer book saved it while other 10th-century copies perished. Sources: Walters Art Museum (`archimedespalimpsest.org`); Cambridge Digital Library (`cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-ADD-01879-00023/1`); Wikipedia ("Archimedes Palimpsest").
- **Cataloging (1899), Heiberg (1906), *Stomachion* and *The Method*.** Cataloged by Papadopoulos-Kerameus in 1899; inspected and photographed in Constantinople by Johan Ludvig Heiberg in 1906. *Stomachion* combinatorics: Reviel Netz and colleagues (2003), with Bill Cutler / Persi Diaconis / Ronald Graham confirming 17,152 square assemblies (536 excluding rotations and reflections). *The Method of Mechanical Theorems*: slicing curved solids into thin strips and balancing them on a lever, addressed to Eratosthenes. Sources: Wikipedia ("Archimedes Palimpsest", "Ostomachion", "The Method of Mechanical Theorems"); MacTutor.
- **Disappearance (~1922), Sirieix, post-1938 forged portraits, and Christie's auction (October 1998).** Acquired in the 1920s by Paris businessman Marie Louis Sirieix; mold damage; four forged medieval evangelist portraits in gold leaf added after 1938 (phthalocyanine pigment postdates 1938; copied from Henri Omont's 1929 book). Sold October 1998 at Christie's New York for $2 million hammer price to an anonymous American buyer and loaned to the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore. Sources: Walters Art Museum; *Physics World* (Uwe Bergmann, Nov. 2007); Wikipedia ("Archimedes Palimpsest").
- **Conservation, multispectral imaging, and Stanford X-ray fluorescence (2005).** Abigail Quandt spent four years separating the leaves; multispectral imaging recovered ~80% of the text in pseudocolor; Uwe Bergmann at SLAC / SSRL began synchrotron X-ray fluorescence scanning in May 2005 (~12 hours per page, hair-thin beam mapping iron fluorescence through the gold-leaf paint and revealing Myronas's 1229 colophon). Sources: Uwe Bergmann, "Archimedes brought to light," *Physics World* (Nov. 2007), via `archimedespalimpsest.org`; Wikipedia ("Archimedes Palimpsest", "Uwe Bergmann").
- **March 2026 Blois leaf rediscovery.** Victor Gysembergh (CNRS / Sorbonne Université), *Zeitschrift für Papyrologie und Epigraphik* vol. 236 (6 March 2026), identified leaf 123 (one of three leaves photographed by Heiberg in 1906 that were missing in 1998) at the Musée des Beaux-Arts de Blois (held there since 1971), carrying *On the Sphere and Cylinder* Book I, Propositions 39–41 on one side and a 20th-century painting of Daniel and two lions on the back; two leaves photographed by Heiberg remain missing. Sources: CNRS press release (9 March 2026); Wikipedia ("Archimedes Palimpsest").
