Eureka

If you know only one thing about Archimedes, it is almost certainly that he once got into a bath, noticed the water sloshing over the side, and was so pleased with what this suggested to him that he leaped out and ran home through the streets of Syracuse stark naked, shouting heurēka, Greek for "I have found it." For two thousand years it has stood as our favorite picture of how a scientific idea arrives. Like nearly everything else people think they know about Archimedes, it suffers from two small defects. Nobody wrote it down until the Roman architect Vitruvius told it nearly two centuries after Archimedes was dead, and the test Vitruvius describes would not have worked.

The puzzle itself sounds real enough. Hiero II, ruler of Syracuse on the eastern coast of Sicily, had given a goldsmith a weighed lump of pure gold to make into a ceremonial wreath for a temple. The finished wreath weighed the right amount, and then word reached Hiero that the smith had pocketed part of the gold and made up the weight with cheaper silver. Hiero wanted to know whether he had been cheated without melting down an offering to the gods. In Vitruvius's version, the bath gave Archimedes his answer: fill a pot to the brim, lower in pure gold matching the wreath's weight, and measure the overflow; refill the pot, lower in the wreath, and measure again. Silver is lighter than gold, bulk for bulk, so an adulterated wreath would be slightly plumper and spill more water. According to Vitruvius, it did, and the goldsmith had a very bad afternoon.

For sixteen centuries everyone nodded along, until in 1586 a twenty-two-year-old Galileo Galilei worked out the arithmetic. A Greek gold wreath was a delicate thing of beaten leaves, weighing perhaps a pound or two. Even if a daring smith had swapped a quarter of the gold for silver, the extra bulk comes to roughly two teaspoons. In a basin wide enough to hold a crown, two extra teaspoons raise the water by less than half a millimeter, hopeless to measure once surface tension and the drops clinging to the metal leaves have had their say. In a short tract called La Bilancetta ("The Little Balance"), Galileo noted that Archimedes himself had discovered a much neater law in On Floating Bodies: water pushes upward on any submerged object with a force equal to the weight of the water it displaces. Hang the wreath from one end of a balance beam and an equal weight of pure gold from the other, and lower the beam into water. The bulkier adulterated wreath gets a firmer upward nudge and tilts the beam at once. Whether Archimedes ever jogged home without his tunic is lost to history, though I like to think he at least had a workable experiment in mind.

We know frustratingly little about what Archimedes was like the rest of the time, when dressed. A friend named Heracleides is said to have written his biography, which was promptly lost. Even his birth date, around 287 BC, rests on a remark by a cheerfully unreliable twelfth-century Byzantine writer that he lived to seventy-five, counted backward from his death in 212 BC. In his surviving works Archimedes drops just one personal clue, and then by accident: discussing estimates of the size of the Sun, he notes in passing what "Phidias my father" had reckoned, and gets straight back to geometry. Plutarch, writing three hundred years later, calls him a relative and friend of King Hiero, whereas other Roman authors call him a man of modest birth. About all we know for certain is that he lived in Syracuse, thought about mathematics from morning till night, and must have been an exhausting person to keep house for.

Plutarch reports that he regularly forgot to eat, and that his servants had to haul him to the bathhouse by force to wash him and rub him down with olive oil. Once there, he sat scraping diagrams into the cold ashes of the stove, and when the attendants oiled him up he used his fingernail to draw lines and circles across his own stomach. Plutarch also claims, with the approving purr of a gentleman who regarded manual trades as beneath notice, that Archimedes considered practical engineering "sordid and ignoble" and refused to leave a written word on the subject. That tells us a great deal about Plutarch and very little about Archimedes, who built machines all his life and even wrote a treatise, now lost, on constructing mechanical planetariums.

For serious mathematical talk he looked across the water to Alexandria, whose great library we met in the previous chapter. For decades he mailed his treatises to Egypt as letters, first to his friend the astronomer Conon of Samos, and after Conon's death to Conon's pupil Dositheus and the librarian Eratosthenes. Reading these cover letters is the closest we get to hearing Archimedes talk, and he had a wonderfully dry, slightly wicked streak. He liked to mail out the bare statements of his new theorems first, saving the proofs for later. Certain mathematicians in Alexandria, it emerged, were reading his letters and airily announcing that they had found these results themselves, without ever showing how. So in one packet Archimedes quietly slipped two theorems that were completely false, "so that those who claim to discover everything, but produce no proofs of the same," as he explained cheerfully to Dositheus in On Spirals, "may be confuted as having pretended to discover the impossible." History does not record who walked into the trap, which is almost a pity.

At home in Syracuse, Hiero treated him as a one-man ministry of marvels. Having proved from geometry why a lever multiplies force (ten pounds ten feet from a pivot balances a hundred pounds one foot out), Archimedes boasted, in a line preserved five centuries later by Pappus of Alexandria, "Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth." Hiero asked to see a small force move something absurd. According to Plutarch, Archimedes chose a three-masted merchant ship that had taken a crowd of men to drag ashore, had it loaded with cargo and crew, sat comfortably at a distance, and winched the vessel smoothly across the ground with one hand through a rig of compound pulleys. He also got lasting credit for the revolving wooden spiral tube still called Archimedes' screw, used for pumping out ships and watering fields, though versions of it had probably irrigated Egypt and Mesopotamia before he was born.

What Archimedes loved above all else, though, was measuring curved things in a world that only knew how to measure straight ones.

Any shape with flat sides is straightforward: chop it into triangles and add their areas. A circle refuses to cooperate because no part of its rim is straight. To measure curves without sloppy claims about infinity, Archimedes perfected an idea begun by Eudoxus of Cnidus and known today as the method of exhaustion, a name that nicely captures both what it does to the gap and what it did to the person calculating it. Trap the curved shape between two flat-sided shapes you can measure, one fitted tightly inside and one wrapped tightly outside, and keep doubling the sides until the space between the two fences is too skinny to matter.

In a crisp three-proposition work called Measurement of a Circle, he turned this vise on π, the distance around a circle of width 1. Sketch a regular six-sided hexagon inside the circle with its corners on the rim. Joining those corners to the center makes six equilateral triangles, each side equal to the radius of 1/2, so walking all six sides covers 3. Since a straight line is the shortest route between two points, the circle curving outside must be longer than 3. Next draw a regular hexagon outside the circle, each flat edge kissing the rim; right triangles put its perimeter at 2√3, about 3.46. Before lunch, π is trapped between 3 and 3.46.

Now comes the heroic part. Halve each arc to turn both hexagons into twelve-sided polygons, and use the Pythagorean theorem to work out the new perimeters from the old. Then double again to twenty-four sides, forty-eight, and ninety-six. Pause and remember what Archimedes lacked: no zero, no decimal point, no algebraic symbols, and nothing like long multiplication on paper. Greek numbers were written with letters of the alphabet, making arithmetic a ferocious workout. At every step he pulled square roots out of awkward fractions, starting from the mysteriously sharp bounds 265/153 and 1351/780 for √3 (how he found them he never bothered to say), while rounding every remainder the honest way, nudging the inner figure slightly down and the outer slightly up so his trap could never cheat. At ninety-six sides, polygons that would look round on a dinner plate, he had squeezed π into a tight box:

In mixed numbers, π is bigger than three and ten seventy-firsts (about 3.1408) and smaller than three and a seventh (about 3.1429), nailing 3.14159 to two decimal places. Anyone who has ever used 22/7 is borrowing Archimedes' outer fence. By the same double-edged logic, showing an unknown area or volume could be neither a whisker bigger nor smaller than a neat fraction without trespassing across a polygon, he found the area under a parabola and proved the result he prized above everything else he ever did: nestle a sphere inside the snuggest cylinder that holds it, lid and base included, and the sphere takes up precisely two-thirds of the cylinder's volume and two-thirds of its surface area.

Left to himself, Archimedes would happily have spent his seventies mailing puzzles to Egypt. Politics intervened. Old Hiero died in 215 BC after half a century at peace with Rome, his heirs rashly backed Carthage in the Second Punic War, and in 214 BC a Roman fleet and army arrived under Marcus Claudius Marcellus expecting a brisk campaign.

What met them was remembered with a shudder by Polybius, born within a generation of the siege, and retold by Livy and Plutarch. Years earlier Hiero had talked Archimedes into designing defensive artillery just in case. Long-range catapults lobbed boulders into Roman galleys far out at sea; when captains rowed in close hoping to duck under their arc, short-range scorpions spat iron bolts through narrow slots in the stone. Worst of all was the Claw of Archimedes, an iron grappling hook dangling from a crane beam over the sea wall. Dropped onto a ship's prow and yanked upward by counterweights inside, it hoisted the bow clean out of the water until the vessel stood nearly vertical on its stern, shook the crew into the harbor like dice from a cup, and let the hull crash onto the rocks. Soon the Romans were such wrecks that whenever a bit of rope or timber showed above the wall, soldiers yelled that Archimedes was aiming some fresh contraption at them and bolted. Marcellus, joking ruefully that he was fighting a "geometrical Briareus," after the hundred-armed monster of myth, called off the assaults and settled in to starve Syracuse out.

Centuries later a grander rumor attached itself to the siege: that Archimedes had lined the walls with mirrors and focused sunlight onto the Roman fleet until ships burst into flame. It is almost certainly bunk. Polybius, Livy and Plutarch, who delighted in cataloging Archimedes' mechanical horrors, say not a word about mirrors, and two of them describe night attacks. Clear accounts of burning mirrors turn up only around AD 500, seven hundred years late. Enthusiasts have tested the notion anyway: in 1973 a Greek engineer marshaled seventy sailors with copper-coated mirrors near Athens and set a tarred rowboat smoking at fifty meters, and MIT students raised small flames on a moored hull in 2005, before a second try on San Francisco Bay mostly warmed the paint. Even at high noon, it asks dozens of men to hold glints steady on a ship sitting obligingly still beneath walls already stocked with catapults and flaming pitch.

In the end, Syracuse fell not to engineering but to wine. One night in 212 BC, during a festival of Artemis when the garrison had drunk too generously, Roman troops scaled a poorly watched tower and poured inside. Marcellus gave strict orders that Archimedes be spared, an optimistic instruction during a sack. Plutarch honorably gives three incompatible accounts of how a soldier killed him anyway: hunched over a diagram and telling the Roman to wait until he finished his proof; pleading in vain with a swordsman not to leave a problem untidy; or carrying sundials and brass instruments to Marcellus in a box that soldiers mistook for gold. His famous curtain line, "Do not disturb my circles," appears in neither Livy nor Plutarch; its closest ancient cousin comes from the first-century writer Valerius Maximus, who has him spreading his hands over the dust and saying, "I beg of you, do not disturb this."

Marcellus shunned the killer, honored Archimedes' kin, and kept just one souvenir from Syracuse: a geared bronze sphere Archimedes had built to reproduce the motions and eclipses of the Sun, Moon and five known planets, an ancestor of the corroded clockwork pulled from a shipwreck off Antikythera in 1901. Friends buried Archimedes outside the city beneath a stone sphere inside a cylinder, as he had asked, and Syracuse then remarkably forgot where he was. When Cicero served in Sicily in 75 BC, local dignitaries assured him that Archimedes' grave was not there at all. Cicero waded into the thorn bushes outside the Agrigentine Gate anyway, spotted a small pillar poking above the brambles with a sphere and cylinder on top, sent men in with sickles, and read the half-worn Greek verses on the base. He could not resist gloating that it took a man from a small Italian hill town to show Syracuse where it had mislaid its greatest citizen. Once Cicero left, the weeds closed back in for good, and nobody today knows where the tomb stood.

His books very nearly vanished with it, and how they survived is one of the oddest relay races in history.

Around AD 950 a scribe in Constantinople copied seven of Archimedes' treatises onto parchment in dark brown iron-gall ink. After Crusaders ransacked Constantinople in 1204, that volume drifted to Jerusalem, where a priest named Ioannes Myronas needed parchment for a prayer book and had no money for fresh skins. Myronas unbound the Archimedes codex along with six other old books, scraped and washed the ink off, cut each sheet in half, turned it sideways, and wrote church prayers over the scrubbed geometry, finishing in April 1229. People sometimes talk about Myronas as if he had committed a crime against civilization, which gets it backward. Every other tenth-century copy of those Archimedes texts perished in wars and fires. Myronas's prayer book sat respected on monastery shelves, at Mar Saba in the Judean desert and later back in Constantinople, for nearly seven centuries. By trying to recycle Archimedes, Myronas saved his life.

In 1899 a cataloger copied down a few faint lines of the sideways undertext, and in 1906 a sharp-eyed Danish classicist, Johan Ludvig Heiberg, caught a train to Constantinople. Sitting by a window with a magnifying glass, Heiberg found two treatises everyone thought had perished in antiquity. One was the Stomachion, on a fourteen-piece square puzzle; nearly a century later Reviel Netz and colleagues realized Archimedes was counting how many ways the pieces form a square (17,152, or 536 ignoring flips and turns). The stunner was The Method of Mechanical Theorems, a letter to Eratosthenes revealing how Archimedes privately found his answers before proving them by exhaustion: he mentally sliced curved solids into infinitely thin wafers and balanced them on an imaginary lever against known shapes. Had that letter surfaced in 1500 instead of 1906, the calculus we shall meet in chapters 9 and 10 might have had a two-century head start.

Having survived Romans, Crusaders and a scraping knife, the book then nearly lost to a French cellar. Around 1922 it slipped out of Istanbul into the hands of a Paris businessman, Marie Louis Sirieix, in whose damp care purple mold chewed holes through the parchment. Worse, sometime after 1938, somebody hoping to smarten the tatty volume up for collectors painted four fake medieval evangelist portraits in thick gold leaf straight over four pages of Archimedes (betrayed later by a modern pigment and a 1929 art book). When Sirieix's family auctioned the wreck at Christie's in New York in October 1998, an anonymous American buyer paid $2 million at the hammer and lent it to the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore.

Conservators spent four years separating the glue-caked leaves. Multispectral cameras recovered four-fifths of the book, coloring the prayers black and Archimedes' ghost lines red, until the forger's gold leaf blocked the light. Salvation came from Uwe Bergmann, a Stanford physicist who usually X-rayed spinach proteins and meteorites, and who read in a magazine that the tenth-century scribe had used iron-gall ink. Starting in May 2005, a hair-thin synchrotron X-ray beam scanned the painted pages; trace iron in the parchment glowed right back through the paint, building the hidden Greek up dot by dot at twelve hours a page and turning up Myronas's 1229 signature for good measure.

Even now, the book is still straggling in. Three leaves Heiberg photographed in 1906 were missing when the codex reached Baltimore. In March 2026 a French researcher, Victor Gysembergh, paging through digitized museum catalogs, spotted one in the fine arts museum in Blois, sitting unnoticed since 1971 with On the Sphere and Cylinder on one side and a twentieth-century painting of Daniel and two lions plastered across the back. Two leaves remain loose somewhere in the world, perhaps in a frame or a desk drawer, waiting for someone to look twice. Archimedes spent a lifetime showing that if you hem an elusive thing in carefully enough from both sides, it cannot get away. Hanging on to the pages where he did it has taken two thousand years, a frugal monk, an enterprising forger and a particle accelerator, and we are still two pages short.
