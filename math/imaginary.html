If there is one thing mathematicians are spectacularly bad at, it is naming things. You would think a profession built on precision, rigor, and a near-pathological hatred of ambiguity would excel at terminology. History suggests the opposite. They have given us numbers called "natural," "real," "rational," and "imaginary," and managed to make every single one of those words misleading.

For a very long time, numbers were reassuringly simple. If you were a prehistoric herdsman, you had sheep. You could have one sheep, two sheep, or three sheep. These are the Natural numbers. They are intuitive, comforting, and have never caused anyone a genuine philosophical crisis. Mathematicians call them "natural" because they feel like the numbers the universe came pre-installed with, the sort you could find growing wild in a meadow if meadows contained arithmetic.

But humans are constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone. Eventually someone realized you could have half a sheep — usually after dinner — and fractions were born. These became known as Rational numbers: any number expressible as a tidy ratio of two whole numbers. Pythagoras and his followers were fanatical about them. To the Pythagoreans, rational numbers were not merely a mathematical convenience — they were the architecture of the cosmos. Music, astronomy, geometry: all of it, they believed, ultimately resolved into perfect numerical ratios. This was a lovely idea. It was also wrong.

The trouble arrived when someone tried to calculate the length of the diagonal of a perfectly ordinary 1-by-1 square. The answer is the square root of 2, which goes on forever without repeating and cannot be expressed as a ratio of any two whole numbers. The Pythagoreans, confronted with this finding, did not update their model. They threw the man who discovered it overboard. Hippasus of Metapontum was drowned at sea, according to legend, for the crime of being correct. They called numbers like his Irrational — meaning "without ratio" — though the name now conjures something closer to a tantrum than a theorem. This is worth sitting with for a moment. There are, it turns out, infinitely more irrational numbers than rational ones. The universe that Pythagoras believed was built from harmonious fractions is, at almost every point, made of something he would have wanted to drown.

Then there is zero, which had its own tortured arrival in Europe. For centuries, European mathematicians simply refused to accept it as a legitimate number. The problem was philosophical: how could nothing be something? The Catholic Church was reportedly suspicious of it on theological grounds. Zero eventually crept into European mathematics in the 12th and 13th centuries, smuggled in from Arabic and Indian scholarship, where it had been working perfectly well for ages. Even so, some Italian cities legally banned merchants from using it in their accounts, on the grounds that it made fraud too easy. A number so threatening to the established order that it was once regulated by commerce law.

But the most dramatic chapter in bad mathematical naming begins in 16th-century Italy, with a feud so petty it would make the Bernoullis wince. A mathematician named Scipione del Ferro solved a general cubic equation — a significant achievement — and then kept it entirely secret, telling only his student on his deathbed, presumably for maximum effect. That student, Antonio Fior, used the secret to issue a public challenge to a rival named Niccolo Tartaglia. Tartaglia, in considerable panic, independently rediscovered the solution the night before the contest and won so thoroughly that Fior disappeared from mathematical history without so much as a footnote.

Then along came Gerolamo Cardano. Cardano was a brilliant, volatile, magnificently difficult man — a physician, a gambler, an astrologer, and one of the most prolific writers of the Renaissance — who heard about Tartaglia's formula and wanted it desperately. He begged. He flattered. He eventually extracted the secret from Tartaglia under a solemn oath of confidentiality, then published it in his 1545 masterwork, the Ars Magna. Tartaglia was incandescent. What followed was years of vicious pamphlets, public accusations of plagiarism, and formal challenges to mathematical combat. It was, essentially, the 16th century's version of an extremely ugly online argument, except slower, and with more Latin.

The reason this matters for our story is that while working through these cubic equations, Cardano kept bumping into the square roots of negative numbers. He called them "sophistic" and "useless," and then immediately used them to get the right answer. He was, in other words, solving real equations by temporarily invoking numbers he considered fictional. This is the mathematical equivalent of lying your way to the truth.

It was Cardano's younger contemporary Rafael Bombelli who finally looked at these quantities without blinking. In 1572, Bombelli published a treatise in which he set out coherent arithmetic rules for working with square roots of negative numbers, treating them as straightforwardly as any other. His contemporaries were baffled. He was, of course, entirely right. But it would take another century and a half for anyone of sufficient authority to say so in public.

That authority arrived, unfortunately, in the form of René Descartes, the 17th-century philosopher who gave us the coordinate plane, analytic geometry, and the existential crisis of "I think, therefore I am." Descartes looked at these square roots of negative numbers and dismissed them with a sneer. He coined the term Imaginary as a deliberate slight — the mathematical equivalent of putting "interesting" in air quotes. He thought they were fictions, curiosities, the kind of thing that appeared in equations the way smudges appeared on otherwise clean manuscripts: to be noted, and ignored.

The derogatory name stuck, as derogatory names tend to. Fortunately, the ever-prolific Leonhard Euler came along in the 18th century, shrugged, and said, "Let's just call it i." He treated imaginary numbers like any other tool, applied them to problem after problem, and watched the universe quietly solve itself. Along the way, he discovered that raising e to the power of i times pi gives you negative one:

eiπ = −1
Euler's Identity

This result, now called Euler's Identity, connects five of the most fundamental constants in all of mathematics — e, i, π, 1, and 0 — in a single, startling equation. Mathematicians reliably vote it the most beautiful result in the discipline. That it was built on a number coined as an insult says something important, though it is not entirely clear what.

By the early 19th century, Carl Friedrich Gauss had had enough. Gauss — by most measures the finest mathematician since Newton — found the word "imaginary" an active embarrassment. He proposed calling them complex numbers instead, pairing a real part with an imaginary one and plotting the result as a point on a two-dimensional plane. This was elegant, geometrically intuitive, and a vast improvement on three hundred years of sneering. He wrote that the "true metaphysics" of these quantities had been obscured by "an unfortunate choice of terminology" — which is Gauss for: Descartes, what on earth were you thinking?

By the time Augustin-Louis Cauchy had erected an entire rigorous theory of complex analysis, the argument was over. Complex numbers were not imaginary in any sense worth worrying about. They turned out to be essential to quantum mechanics, the mathematics of electrical engineering, the behavior of waves, the modelling of fluid dynamics, and the design of the alternating current that runs through the walls of every building you have ever been in. Your smartphone performs complex arithmetic thousands of times per second without either of you giving it much thought.

To distinguish everyday numbers from Descartes's supposedly fictitious ones, mathematicians retroactively named the familiar ones Real numbers. So the final taxonomy, assembled over two millennia of brilliant and occasionally homicidal mathematicians, gives us this: "Real" numbers, which do not physically exist anywhere; "Imaginary" numbers, which quietly underpin the modern world; "Irrational" numbers, which require no therapy whatsoever; and "Natural" numbers, which are the only ones you would actually encounter in nature, assuming nature had opinions about the number seven.

It is a classification system that would embarrass a zoologist. It is what happens when the people who discover things are not, by disposition, the people who should be naming them.

The larger lesson, if you are inclined to draw one, is that mathematics has always proceeded this way: a new kind of number appears, someone important calls it useless or offensive or simply wrong, and then a generation or two later someone else quietly uses it to build something indispensable. Hippasus was drowned for irrational numbers. Bombelli was ridiculed for imaginary ones. Zero was banned. If past form is anything to go by, whatever today's mathematicians are currently dismissing as absurd is probably going to end up in your next phone.
