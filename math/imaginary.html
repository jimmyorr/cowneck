An Imaginary Problem

If there is one thing mathematicians are worse at than conflict resolution, it is naming things. You would think a profession dedicated to absolute precision would excel at terminology, but history suggests otherwise. They have named numbers "natural," "real," "rational," and "imaginary," and managed to make almost all of those words misleading.

For a long time, numbers were simple. If you were a prehistoric herdsman, you had sheep. You could have one sheep, two sheep, or three sheep. These are the Natural numbers. They are intuitive, comforting, and frankly, they never hurt anyone. Mathematicians love to call them "natural" because they feel like the numbers the universe came with, as if you could find them growing wild in a field.

But humans are never satisfied. Soon they realized you could have half a sheep (usually after dinner), which gave birth to fractions. Mathematicians eventually termed these Rational numbers, meaning any number that can be expressed as a neat, tidy ratio. The ancient Greeks, especially Pythagoras and his cult of followers, loved rational numbers. They believed the universe was built entirely on perfect, harmonious ratios. Numbers, to a Pythagorean, were not just a tool for counting. They were a religion.

This worldview worked wonderfully until someone tried to find the length of the diagonal of a 1-by-1 square. They discovered the square root of 2, a number that goes on forever without repeating and cannot possibly be written as a neat ratio. The Pythagoreans were so deeply offended by this mathematical monstrosity that, according to legend, they threw the man who proved its existence (Hippasus of Metapontum) off a boat and drowned him. They called these numbers Irrational. Today, calling a number "irrational" sounds like it's throwing a tantrum in a grocery store, but it simply means "without ratio." There are, it turns out, far more irrational numbers than rational ones. The universe is mostly made of things the Pythagoreans would have wanted to drown.

Then there is zero. You might assume zero was always welcome, but it was not. For centuries, European mathematicians refused to accept it as a real number at all. It was too philosophically uncomfortable. How can nothing be something? The Catholic Church was reportedly suspicious of it on theological grounds. Zero only crept into mainstream European mathematics in the 12th and 13th centuries, imported from Arabic and Indian scholars who had sensibly been using it for ages. Even then, some Italian merchants were legally forbidden from using it in their accounting books, because it was too easy to commit fraud by adding zeroes. A number so radical that it was once banned by accountants.

But the story of the most controversial numbers of all begins in 16th-century Italy, with a feud so petty it would make the Bernoullis blush. A mathematician named Scipione del Ferro had secretly solved a type of cubic equation, but told no one except his student on his deathbed, presumably for dramatic effect. That student, Antonio Fior, then used the secret to challenge a mathematician named Niccolo Tartaglia to a public contest. Tartaglia, in a panic, figured out the solution himself the night before the contest, then won so comprehensively that Fior dropped out of history entirely.

Then along came Gerolamo Cardano, a brilliant and magnificently difficult man who was also a gambler, astrologer, and prolific self-promoter. Cardano begged Tartaglia to share his cubic formula, swore a solemn oath of secrecy, then published it anyway in his 1545 masterwork, the Ars Magna. Tartaglia was furious. The resulting feud produced decades of vicious pamphlets, public insults, and formal challenges to mathematical duels. It was the 16th century's version of a very ugly comment thread.

The reason any of this matters for our story is that in working through these cubic equations, Cardano began encountering the square roots of negative numbers. He called them "sophistic" and "useless," then used them anyway to get the right answer. He was basically solving equations by using a number he thought was fictional to arrive at a number he believed was real. This is the mathematical equivalent of lying to get to the truth.

It was Cardano's contemporary Rafael Bombelli who finally looked at these strange creatures without flinching. In 1572, Bombelli published a book in which he simply defined rules for calculating with square roots of negative numbers as if they were perfectly normal. His contemporaries thought he was unhinged. He was, in fact, right. But it took another century and a half before anyone important would admit it.

Enter René Descartes, the 17th-century philosopher who gave us the coordinate plane and the existential crisis of "I think therefore I am." When Descartes looked at these negative square roots, he essentially rolled his eyes. He coined the term Imaginary as a deliberate insult. He thought they were fictitious, useless, and essentially mathematical garbage. He dropped the word into his work the way you might describe a colleague's idea as "very creative" at a meeting.

The derogatory name stuck, mostly because mathematicians are terrible at rebranding. Fortunately, the ever-prolific Leonhard Euler came along in the 18th century, shrugged, and said, "Let's just call it i." He treated these so-called imaginary numbers like normal numbers, and suddenly they were solving impossible problems left and right. He also discovered that if you raised the number e to the power of i times pi, you got negative one:

eiπ = −1
Euler's Identity

This relationship, now called Euler's Identity, links the most fundamental constants in mathematics in a single equation. Mathematicians routinely vote it the most beautiful equation ever discovered, which is a lot to ask of a number that Descartes invented specifically to mock.

By the early 19th century, Carl Friedrich Gauss had finally had enough of the whole "imaginary" debacle. Gauss was, by any measure, one of the greatest mathematicians who ever lived, and he found the name an embarrassment. He proposed calling them complex numbers instead, combining a real part and an imaginary part into a single object, and representing them as points on a two-dimensional plane. This was elegant, sensible, and a genuine improvement. He said the "imaginary" label had caused "an unexpected objection" and that the "true metaphysics of these quantities" had been obscured by unfortunate terminology. Which is the 19th-century academic's way of saying: Descartes, what were you thinking?

By the time Augustin-Louis Cauchy had built an entire rigorous theory around complex numbers, the verdict was in. They were not imaginary in any meaningful sense. They described how electric fields propagate through space. They were essential to quantum mechanics. They turned up in the mathematics of fluid flow, signal processing, and the theory of vibrations. The engineers who designed the alternating current that powers your refrigerator relied on them. Your smartphone uses complex numbers thousands of times per second without either of you finding it particularly fictitious.

But the damage was done. To distinguish the normal, everyday numbers from Descartes's so-called "imaginary" ones, mathematicians retroactively named the regular numbers Real numbers. So now we are stuck with a system where "Real" numbers sound like they physically exist in nature (they don't; you can't hold a "3" in your hand), "Imaginary" numbers sound like fairy dust (even though they run the modern world), "Irrational" numbers sound like they need a therapist, and "Natural" numbers are the only ones you'd actually find in nature, if nature contained things like "seven."

It is a taxonomy that would embarrass a biologist. It is the naming convention of people who expected to be using these numbers privately and never thought they'd have to explain them to anyone. Which, to be fair, is also how most bad naming decisions get made.

The real lesson here, if you will pardon the use of the word "real," is that mathematics has a long history of things that seem impossible turning out to be indispensable. Every time a new kind of number appeared, someone with institutional authority called it useless and offensive, and then a generation later someone else quietly built the future with it. Hippasus drowned for irrational numbers. Bombelli was laughed at for imaginary ones. Zero was banned. If history is any guide, whatever the mathematicians of today are calling absurd is probably going to end up in your next phone.
