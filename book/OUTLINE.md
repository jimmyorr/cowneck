# Outline

**Working title:** *Unspeakable: A Brief and Quarrelsome History of Mathematics*

(The title comes from *alogos*, the Greek word for irrational numbers, which
meant both "without ratio" and "unspeakable." It's a placeholder.)

**Premise:** the history of mathematics told through the people who made it:
their feuds, vanities, misfortunes and terrible naming decisions. It runs
roughly in chronological order, in four parts and 22 chapters. Each chapter
stands on its own as a web page and also reads in sequence. See `STYLE.md` for
voice, accuracy and template rules.

Each chapter entry lists:

- **Owns:** the people and stories this chapter tells in full. No other chapter
  retells them (see `STYLE.md`, "Cross-references").
- **Material:** leads gathered during planning. Items marked *(verify)* are
  shaky or disputed, and those marked *(apocryphal)* are known to be doubtful.
  The chapter session must check everything, not just the marked items.
- **Central idea:** the one piece of mathematics the chapter explains.

## Part One: Counting Sheep

### 1. Notches — `book/notches.html`
- **Owns:** prehistoric tallies, Babylonian and Egyptian mathematics.
- **Material:** the Ishango bone (Congo, about 20,000 years old, found by
  Belgian geologist Jean de Heinzelin in 1950; theories that it records primes
  or a lunar calendar are speculative, which makes good material for gentle
  skepticism). Babylonian base 60, and why an hour still has 60 minutes and a
  circle 360 degrees. Tablet YBC 7289, which gives &radic;2 to about six
  decimal places a thousand years before the Greeks panicked about it (set up
  ch. 2). Plimpton 322, bought from the antiquities dealer Edgar Banks, often
  called a model for Indiana Jones *(verify; hedge)*; the 2017 UNSW claim that
  it is a trigonometric table is disputed by other scholars. The Rhind
  papyrus, bought in Luxor in 1858 by a Scottish lawyer who had gone to Egypt
  for his health.
- **Central idea:** place value. Why "60" and "1" can be written with the same
  wedge, and why that's both clever and confusing.

### 2. Beans and Diagonals — `book/beans.html`
- **Owns:** Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans, Hippasus, Euclid, the library at
  Alexandria, Eratosthenes, Hypatia.
- **Material:** Pythagoras as cult leader. The bean prohibition, and the story
  that he died rather than cross a bean field *(apocryphal)*. Hippasus,
  expelled or drowned (versions vary, all written down centuries later; move
  the full telling here from `math/imaginary.html`). Euclid's *Elements*, the
  most printed book after the Bible *(verify claim)*, and "there is no royal
  road to geometry" *(attributed to Euclid and to Menaechmus; hedge)*.
  Eratosthenes measuring the Earth with a well at Syene and a shadow stick.
  Hypatia's murder in 415 (the facts are grim and the legend inflated; handle
  with care).
- **Central idea:** why &radic;2 can't be a fraction (the proof is short enough
  to sketch in prose).

### 3. Eureka — `book/eureka.html`
- **Owns:** Archimedes, the Archimedes Palimpsest.
- **Material:** the bath and the crown (from Vitruvius, two centuries later;
  hedge). The siege of Syracuse: the "claw" that lifted ships, and burning
  mirrors *(doubtful; many modern attempts have failed)*. His death at the
  hands of a Roman soldier ("Do not disturb my circles" is a later
  embellishment). Cicero finding his overgrown tomb, marked with a sphere and
  cylinder, in 75 BC. The palimpsest: copied around 950, scraped and reused as
  a prayer book in 1229, identified by Heiberg in Constantinople in 1906, then
  lost. A 20th-century dealer forged gospel portraits over some pages to raise
  its value. It sold at Christie's in 1998 for $2 million and was imaged with
  X-rays at the Walters in Baltimore.
- **Central idea:** the method of exhaustion: squeezing &pi; between polygons.

### 4. Nothing Doing — `book/zero.html`
- **Owns:** zero, Brahmagupta, al-Khwarizmi, Fibonacci, the arrival of
  Hindu-Arabic numerals in Europe.
- **Material:** Brahmagupta's rules for zero (628), including his wrong answer
  for 0&divide;0. The Bakhshali manuscript and its disputed 2017 carbon dating.
  The Maya, who invented zero independently. Al-Khwarizmi's *al-jabr* (whose
  title gave us "algebra") and his own name, which gave us "algorithm." His
  *jidhr* (root) becoming *radix*. *Sifr* giving us both "zero" and "cipher."
  Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* (1202); the rabbit problem, which assumes immortal
  rabbits. Florence's 1299 ban on the numerals in bank ledgers. Move the full
  telling here from `math/imaginary.html`.
- **Central idea:** why place value needs a symbol for "nothing here."

## Part Two: Merchants, Magicians and Duelists

### 5. An Imaginary Problem — `math/imaginary.html` *(drafted; needs trimming)*
- **Owns:** del Ferro, Fior, Tartaglia, Cardano, Ferrari, Bombelli, Descartes's
  naming of "imaginary" and "real," Euler's identity, Wessel, Argand, Gauss's
  complex plane.
- **To do in the editing pass:** cut Hippasus (now ch. 2), zero and
  al-Khwarizmi's *jidhr* (now ch. 4) and Rudolff's &radic; (now ch. 6) down to
  a sentence or a pointer each. Rework the opening so it doesn't repeat ch. 2's
  material on natural and rational numbers.

### 6. Signs of the Times — `book/notation.html`
- **Owns:** the history of mathematical symbols.
- **Material:** + and &minus; in German commercial arithmetic (Widmann, 1489).
  Robert Recorde's = in *The Whetstone of Witte* (1557): "no two things can be
  more equal," drawn much longer than ours. He died in a debtors' prison the
  next year, and the sign didn't appear in print again until 1618. Rudolff's
  &radic; (1525). Oughtred's &times; (1631), which Leibniz disliked because it
  looked like *x*. Rahn's &divide; (1659). Why *x* is the unknown: the stories
  about Descartes's printer running out of letters and about Spanish "xei"
  are both *(apocryphal)*, and saying so is the joke. Leibniz the
  notation-obsessive.
- **Central idea:** notation as technology. Try doing long division in Roman
  numerals.

### 7. The Wizard of Merchiston — `book/napier.html`
- **Owns:** John Napier, Henry Briggs, logarithms, the slide rule.
- **Material:** Napier's reputation as a sorcerer: the black rooster dusted
  with soot to catch a thieving servant, and the spider he kept in a box
  *(legend; hedge)*. His *Plaine Discovery of the Whole Revelation of Saint
  John* (1593), which he considered his real life's work and which predicted
  the end of the world *(verify the dates)*. Designs for war machines. Logarithms
  (1614). Briggs riding to Scotland in 1615 and the two men reportedly gazing
  at each other in silent admiration for a quarter of an hour *(verify; hedge)*.
  Kepler using logarithms for planetary tables. The slide rule, which put
  people on the Moon.
- **Central idea:** turning multiplication into addition.

### 8. Dice and the Devil — `book/probability.html`
- **Owns:** Pascal, Fermat (except his Last Theorem, which belongs to ch. 22),
  the Chevalier de Méré, the problem of points, Huygens.
- **Material:** a gambler's question about an interrupted game leads to the
  Pascal–Fermat letters of 1654. Fermat was a lawyer in Toulouse who did
  mathematics as a hobby and published almost nothing. Pascal's calculating
  machine; his "night of fire" (23 November 1654), recorded on a note he sewed
  into his coat; the Wager. Cardano's earlier dice book (one line, pointing
  back to ch. 5).
- **Central idea:** expected value: how to divide the pot fairly.

### 9. Gentlemen, This Is War — `book/newton-leibniz.html`
- **Owns:** Leibniz, the calculus priority dispute, Newton only as a combatant
  in it.
- **Angle:** tell it from **Leibniz's side**. See `STYLE.md` ("Stay out of
  Bryson's books"): Newton's personal life (alchemy, the eye-poking
  experiments, the plague years and the apple, Halley's visit and the
  *Principia*, the Hooke feud, the Mint) is off-limits beyond a passing clause.
- **Material:** Leibniz as a court librarian in Hanover, commissioned to write
  a history of his employers' family, which he spent decades not finishing.
  His Step Reckoner calculating machine, shown to the Royal Society in 1673.
  Binary arithmetic and his excitement over the I Ching. His obsession with
  good notation (&int; and *dx*, which won). Newton hid his method in a 1677
  letter as a scrambled anagram of letters and numbers. The 1712
  *Commercium Epistolicum* that "cleared" Newton was largely drafted by Newton
  himself, anonymously. George I, Leibniz's employer, became King of England
  in 1714 and left Leibniz behind in Hanover. Leibniz died in 1716; his
  funeral was poorly attended *(verify the "only his secretary" claim)*. The
  aftermath: Britain stuck with Newton's dots and fell behind the Continent for
  a century; the Analytical Society's joke about "pure D-ism" versus "the
  Dot-age of the University" *(verify wording; Babbage himself belongs to ch.
  15)*.
- **Central idea:** what a derivative is, using a car's speedometer rather
  than a falling apple.

### 10. The Calculus of Dysfunction — `math/bernoulli.html` *(drafted)*
- **Owns:** Jacob, Johann and Daniel Bernoulli, l'Hôpital, the brachistochrone,
  Euler's life (the only chapter that tells it in full).
- **To do in the editing pass:** check that nothing later retells Euler's
  blindness, the house fire or his death.

### 11. The Witch of Agnesi — `book/agnesi.html`
- **Owns:** Émilie du Châtelet, Maria Gaetana Agnesi, Laura Bassi: how
  calculus and Newton reached continental Europe, often through women
  translating and teaching it.
- **Material:** du Châtelet used her mathematics to win at the gaming tables
  of Versailles *(verify)*, lived with Voltaire at Cirey, and wrote
  *Institutions de physique*. She raced to finish her French translation of
  the *Principia* while pregnant at 42 and died days after giving birth in 1749.
  It is still the standard French translation. Laura Bassi, in 1732, became
  the second woman in Europe to earn a university degree and later a paid
  professor at Bologna. Agnesi's *Instituzioni analitiche* (1748) was one of
  the first comprehensive calculus textbooks. Her curve, the *versiera* ("that
  which turns"), was mistranslated by a Cambridge professor, John Colson, as
  if it were *avversiera* ("she-devil"), which is how English came to call it
  "the witch of Agnesi." That fits the book's running theme of bad names. She
  then gave up mathematics for charity and died in 1799 in the poorhouse she
  ran.
- **Central idea:** the witch curve itself, built from a rotating line and
  a circle. It's simple enough to describe in prose.

## Part Three: Prodigies, Rebels and Lost Causes

### 12. Few, but Ripe — `book/gauss.html`
- **Owns:** Gauss.
- **Material:** summing 1 to 100 as a schoolboy: Sartorius's original account
  has no numbers and no trick, and the familiar details were added much later
  (Brian Hayes, *American Scientist*, 2006). The legend and how it grew is the
  story. The 17-sided polygon at 19, and the request for one on his tombstone
  that a mason supposedly refused because it would look like a circle *(verify;
  hedge)*. Predicting where the lost asteroid Ceres would reappear in 1801. His
  motto *pauca sed matura* ("few, but ripe") and his habit of sitting on
  discoveries, which set up ch. 14. Sophie Germain gets a sentence here, with
  her full story in ch. 18.
- **Central idea:** constructing the 17-gon, or modular arithmetic.
  Pick the one that tells better.

### 13. Young Men in a Hurry — `book/galois-abel.html`
- **Owns:** Galois, Abel, and the impossibility of solving the quintic.
- **Material:** Abel, poor in Norway, proved the quintic unsolvable, had his
  paper ignored or mislaid in Paris, and died of tuberculosis in 1829 at 26. A
  letter offering him a post in Berlin arrived just after he died *(verify the
  timing)*. Galois twice failed the École Polytechnique entrance exam (the
  thrown eraser is *(apocryphal)*). Manuscripts were lost by Cauchy and by
  Fourier, who died holding one. Galois was jailed for republican agitation
  and died after a duel in 1832 at 20. E. T. Bell's legend of the frantic
  final night is mostly invented: the papers were mostly written earlier, and
  "I have no time" appears once, beside one proof. Debunking it is part of the
  story.
- **Central idea:** what it means to prove that something is *impossible*.

### 14. Parallel Lives — `book/non-euclidean.html`
- **Owns:** Euclid's fifth postulate, Saccheri, Farkas and János Bolyai,
  Lobachevsky, Riemann.
- **Material:** two thousand years of attempts to prove the parallel postulate.
  Farkas Bolyai warned his son to leave the problem alone, as he would fear a
  dangerous passion *(get the real wording)*. János did it anyway, and Gauss
  replied that praising it would be praising himself, since he'd thought of it
  first. János was crushed. Lobachevsky published in Russian in a provincial
  journal. Riemann's 1854 lecture, which Gauss chose from three topics and
  which ended up as the geometry of general relativity.
- **Central idea:** triangles whose angles don't add up to 180&deg;, shown on a
  sphere or a saddle.

### 15. The Engine Men — `book/babbage-lovelace.html`
- **Owns:** Charles Babbage, Ada Lovelace.
- **Material:** Babbage's crusade against street musicians. His reported
  letter to Tennyson correcting "every moment dies a man, every moment one is
  born" on statistical grounds *(verify)*. The Difference Engine and the
  government money it consumed; the Analytical Engine. Lovelace, Byron's
  daughter, raised on mathematics to keep the poetry out. Her Note G (1843) is
  a program for computing Bernoulli numbers, so point back to ch. 10. The
  Science Museum built Difference Engine No. 2 in 1991, and it worked.
- **Central idea:** the method of differences: computing tables using only
  addition.

### 16. Lies, Damned Lies — `book/statistics.html`
- **Owns:** Quetelet, Florence Nightingale, Galton, Gosset, Fisher.
- **Material:** Quetelet's "average man." Nightingale's rose diagrams; she was
  the first woman elected to the Royal Statistical Society (1858). Galton
  measured boredom by counting fidgets, made a "beauty map" of Britain by
  pricking a card in his pocket, and analyzed guesses of an ox's weight at a
  fair (1907; verify the figures). Galton also founded eugenics, which needs
  honest, unfunny treatment, and the tone has to shift for it. Gosset
  published as "Student" because Guinness forbade its brewers to publish.
  Fisher and the lady tasting tea (Muriel Bristol).
- **Central idea:** averages and variation, or why one test proves little.

### 17. Paradise — `book/cantor.html`
- **Owns:** Cantor, Kronecker's hostility, Russell's paradox, Frege.
- **Material:** some infinities are bigger than others (the diagonal argument).
  Kronecker's campaign against Cantor. Cantor's depression and repeated stays in
  the Halle sanatorium. His side project arguing that Francis Bacon wrote
  Shakespeare *(verify details)*. He died in 1918, malnourished, in wartime.
  Hilbert: "No one shall expel us from the paradise that Cantor has created."
  Russell's 1902 letter reached Frege as his life's work was going to press,
  and Frege added a heartbroken appendix.
- **Central idea:** the diagonal argument. It fits in a paragraph and a small
  table.

## Part Four: The Modern Muddle

### 18. Not a Bathhouse — `book/women.html`
- **Owns:** Sophie Germain, Sofia Kovalevskaya, Emmy Noether (Hilbert appears
  here as her champion).
- **Material:** Germain taught herself by candlelight against her parents'
  wishes and wrote to Lagrange and Gauss as "Monsieur LeBlanc." She asked a
  French general to keep Gauss safe in 1806 *(verify)*. Kovalevskaya entered a
  fictitious marriage to leave Russia and study, and won the Prix Bordin in
  1888, when the jury raised the prize money. Noether lectured at Göttingen
  under Hilbert's name, and Hilbert's response to colleagues objecting to a
  woman lecturer was "we are a university, not a bathhouse" *(wording varies;
  hedge)*. Noether's theorem; her dismissal in 1933; Bryn Mawr; Einstein's
  obituary letter.
- **Central idea:** Noether's theorem in plain words: symmetry gives
  conservation laws.

### 19. A Letter from Madras — `book/ramanujan.html`
- **Owns:** Ramanujan, Hardy, Littlewood.
- **Material:** the 1913 letter of about 120 theorems, which Hardy nearly
  dismissed. Hardy's private feud with God: the Riemann postcard sent before a
  rough North Sea crossing as "insurance," and his covering of hotel mirrors
  *(verify)*. Cricket. Ramanujan's illness in wartime England. The taxi
  numbered 1729. He died in 1920 at 32. Don't reuse the title *The Man Who Knew
  Infinity*.
- **Central idea:** 1729 and partitions, or why "dull" numbers aren't.

### 20. Incomplete — `book/godel.html`
- **Owns:** Hilbert's program and the 1900 problems, Gödel, the Einstein
  friendship.
- **Material:** Hilbert's 23 problems (Paris, 1900). His 1930 radio address,
  which ended "We must know. We will know," the same week, in the same city,
  that Gödel quietly announced the incompleteness result *(verify the
  timing)*. Gödel and Einstein walking home together at Princeton. His 1947
  citizenship hearing, where he tried to explain a flaw in the Constitution.
  His fear of poisoning; he died in 1978 weighing about 65 pounds.
- **Central idea:** "This statement cannot be proved." Incompleteness in plain
  words.

### 21. The Imitation Game — `book/turing.html`
- **Owns:** Alan Turing.
- **Material:** the 1936 paper and the halting problem. Bletchley Park and
  Enigma. He ran marathon-standard times (about 2:46), chained his mug to a
  radiator, and fixed a slipping bicycle chain by counting pedal turns *(verify)*.
  His 1952 conviction and chemical castration. His death in 1954: the apple is
  famous, the inquest's verdict is disputed, and the chapter should say so.
  The 2009 apology, the 2013 pardon, the £50 note in 2021. Pick a different
  title, since *The Imitation Game* is a film.
- **Central idea:** why no program can decide whether every program halts.

### 22. Unclaimed Prizes — `book/fermat-perelman.html`
- **Owns:** Fermat's Last Theorem, Wiles, Wolfskehl, Erdős, Perelman.
- **Material:** Fermat's 1637 margin note, published by his son in 1670. The
  Wolfskehl Prize of 1908, and the story that it saved its founder from
  suicide *(apocryphal; hedge)*. Wiles worked for seven years in his attic and
  announced in Cambridge in 1993, then a gap was found; he fixed it with Richard
  Taylor in 1994. Erdős lived out of a suitcase, said "my brain is open," and
  won Ron Graham's $500 bet that he couldn't stop amphetamines for a month,
  complaining that he'd set mathematics back a month; he offered cash prizes
  for problems. Perelman declined the Fields Medal (2006) and the Clay
  Institute's $1 million (2010). The book ends on mathematicians who didn't
  want the reward, a quiet close for the whole book.
- **Central idea:** what Fermat claimed, and why a "proof" can be wrong for a
  year.

## Process

1. **Chapter sessions.** One session per chapter. Each one reads `STYLE.md`,
   this outline and one finished chapter. It researches with web searches,
   writes `book/<slug>.html` and `book/notes/<slug>.md`, and pushes to its own
   branch. Sessions can run in parallel because each touches only its own two
   files.
2. **Editing pass.** One session reads every chapter in order. It cuts
   repetition, enforces ownership, adds back-references, trims ch. 5 as noted,
   and reads every hedge in the notes files.
3. **Front matter.** A contents page (`book/index.html`), probably a short
   preface, and a projects-page card.
