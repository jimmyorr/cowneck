## Sources by claim

**Black cockerel and soot.** From Mark Napier, *Memoirs of John Napier of Merchiston* (Edinburgh: Blackwood, 1834), as summarized by MacTutor and Wikipedia (John Napier). Mark Napier offers the soot trick as a rational explanation of the sorcery tradition. Hedged in the text as a family story written down more than 200 years later.

**Spider in a box.** Wikipedia (John Napier); St Andrews University Collections blog "Marvellous Merchiston" (2017); scihi.org. One retelling says an ivory box. Hedged ("He was said to…"). The original source is unknown.

**Drunken pigeons.** Mark Napier (1834) via MacTutor/Wikipedia. Some later retellings say the pigeons belonged to the Lord of Roslin and that the peas were soaked in brandy; I left out the neighbor's name and the brandy as unverifiable. Hedged ("is supposed to," "according to the family tradition").

**Logan of Restalrig contract, 1594.** Wikipedia (Fast Castle); clan.com "The story of Fast Castle." The quotation "do his utmost diligence to search and seek out, and by all craft and ingine to find out the same" and the one-third share come from these. Logan's posthumous implication in the Gowrie conspiracy is behind the remark about conspiracies. I could not confirm the month, so the text gives only the year.

**Birth 1550, father aged 16, Bothwell letter, St Andrews at 13, return 1571 fluent in Greek.** Wikipedia (John Napier). The Bothwell letter is dated 5 December 1560. Napier was born 1 February 1550 per Merchiston Tower (Wikipedia).

**Two marriages, twelve children.** Encyclopedia.com: Elizabeth Stirling (married 1572, two children) and Agnes Chisholm (ten children).

**Salt as fertilizer, hydraulic screw for coal pits.** Encyclopedia.com (DSB entry); MacTutor DNB entry.

**Plaine Discovery (1593).** It names the Pope as Antichrist. The dedication to James VI urges a purge of "papists, atheists and newtrals" (National Library of Scotland rare-books page; Friends of the National Libraries). The quotation "The day of Gods judgement appears to fall betwixt the yeares of Christ, 1688. and 1700." is from proposition 14 (quotepark citation; Cambridge, *Scottish Journal of Theology*, "John Napier and the mathematics of the middle future apocalypse"). The text modernizes the spelling to "years" and keeps "judgement" inside the quotation. On translations and editions: the 1611 preface mentions French and Dutch translations; a German edition appeared at Frankfurt in 1615 and a French one at La Rochelle in 1605. On its being his most widely published work: NLS and FNL. The line "If you had asked an educated European in 1610…" is the narrator's inference, not a sourced claim.

**Secret Inventions (7 June 1596).** MacTutor and Wikipedia quotations: burning mirrors, artillery to "clear a field of four miles circumference of all living creatures exceeding a foot of height," the chariot as "a moving mouth of mettle," devices "of sayling under water." The text keeps the quoted spelling as given in those sources.

**Logarithms: 1614 *Descriptio*, 57 pages of text and 90 of tables, "logos" + "arithmos," about twenty years of work.** Wikipedia (*Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio*). The twenty years is Napier's own estimate as commonly reported, hedged as "by his own reckoning, something like." Wikipedia's claim that he computed "almost ten million entries" was left out as doubtful.

**Ratio 0.9999999, two moving points, log of 10,000,000 = 0, the "Napierian logarithm" misnomer.** Wikipedia (Napierian logarithm); MathWorld. NapLog(x) = 10^7 ln(10^7/x), so it is related to the natural log but is not the natural log.

**Worked example.** 16 × 256 = 4096; 2^4 × 2^8 = 2^12. Checked.

**Briggs's letter to Ussher, 10 March 1615.** MacTutor (Briggs). "Napper, lord of Markinston, hath set my head and hands a work…" The second sentence, "I hope to see him this summer, if it please God, for I never saw book which pleased me better or made me more wonder," is the standard quotation in MacTutor and other sources, but the search snippets confirmed only the first sentence. **Spot-check.**

**Lilly/Marr account.** William Lilly, *History of his Life and Times* (1715 ed.), via MacTutor (Briggs) and the OpenLearn "John Napier" course, section 5. Verbatim: "almost one quarter of an hour was spent, each beholding the other with admiration, before one word was spoken"; "Ah, John (saith Marchiston), Mr Briggs will not now come"; "by what engine of wit or ingenuity you came first to think of this most excellent help unto astronomy, viz. the Logarithms." The "I wonder nobody else found it out before" part is paraphrased, not quoted. The outline's "gazing at each other in silent admiration for a quarter of an hour" is therefore accurate to the source, and the text hedges it as Lilly's secondhand account written decades later.

**Briggs on astrology, "a mere system of groundless conceits."** Wikipedia (Henry Briggs), citing Thomas Gataker.

**Briggs: Gresham 1596, Savilian 1619, died 26 January 1630; visits 1615 and 1616; base 10 with log 1 = 0; tables to 1,000 in 1617; *Arithmetica Logarithmica* 1624 (1–20,000 and 90,000–100,000 to 14 places); Vlacq 1628.** MacTutor (Briggs); Wikipedia (Henry Briggs). Napier's reply that he had thought of base 10 but was too ill comes from Briggs's own preface to the *Arithmetica*, as reported by MacTutor. log10 2 = 0.30103. Checked.

**Napier's death (4 April 1617, gout, at Merchiston, aged 67).** Wikipedia. I left the burial out because sources disagree (St Giles kirkyard, later a vault at St Cuthbert's).

**Rabdologia (1617), Napier's bones, decimal point; *Constructio* published by his son Robert in 1619.** Wikipedia (Rabdology); Encyclopedia.com. Some sources say Rabdologia has the "first printed" decimal point, which overclaims (Clavius, Stevin and others came earlier), so the text says "helped to popularize."

**Bürgi.** Tables printed in Prague in 1620, possibly developed decades earlier, independent of Napier. Clark and Montelle, *Revue d'histoire des mathématiques* 18 (2012); CACM blog; Britannica. Kepler's often-quoted complaint that Bürgi was a "cunctator" (procrastinator) could not be verified and was not used.

**Kepler.** He saw the *Descriptio* in 1617 and was not at first impressed. His letter of congratulation is dated 28 July 1619, after Napier's death, and was printed as the dedication of the 1620 *Ephemerides*. Sources: Encyclopedia.com/DSB; OpenLearn; Cambridge HPS "Kepler and Mathematical Techniques." The *Rudolphine Tables* (1627) were 26 years in the making (Wikipedia, Rudolphine Tables).

**Katharina Kepler.** Accused in 1615 (by Ursula Reinbold), arrested 7 August 1620, held 14 months (about 405 days), threatened with torture (*territio*, shown the instruments, not actually tortured), and freed 4 October 1621. Sources: APS News "August 1620"; Wikipedia (Katharina Kepler). The text says "shown the instruments of torture," which is the accurate version.

**Scottish witch trials in Napier's lifetime.** These include the North Berwick trials (1590) and the 1597 hunt. This is general knowledge; the text keeps the reference vague ("savage witch trials of its own").

**Laplace quotation.** "...by reducing to a few days the labour of many months, doubles the life of the astronomer, and spares him the errors and disgust inseparable from long calculations." From MacTutor's Laplace quotations, from *Exposition du système du monde* (1796). The text shifts the verb to "double … spare" to fit the sentence and keeps the core wording.

**Gunter (about 1620), Oughtred (1622, two sliding scales), Delamain dispute over the circular rule.** MacTutor (Delamain, Oughtred); Wikipedia (Richard Delamain). Some sources date Gunter's scale to 1620 and others to 1623–24, hence "around 1620." Oughtred's × belongs to ch. 6, so it gets a pointer only.

**Apollo slide rules.** Apollo 13's Pickett N600-ES is in the National Air and Space Museum (si.edu object A19840160000). Aldrin's Apollo 11 N600-ES sold at Heritage Auctions in September 2007 for $77,675, with a 1969 retail price of $10.95 (Paul Fraser Collectibles, Just Collecting). The outline's line that the slide rule "put people on the Moon" was softened to the verifiable fact that slide rules flew as backup calculators.

**Merchiston Tower** stands at the center of Edinburgh Napier University's Merchiston campus (Wikipedia, Merchiston Tower; Napier University library blog).

## Unverified or hedged claims

- **Cockerel and soot:** family tradition first recorded in 1834. Hedged.
- **Spider in a box:** source unknown. Hedged ("was said to").
- **Drunken pigeons:** family tradition. Hedged. The neighbor's identity is omitted.
- **Lilly/Marr meeting:** secondhand, written decades later, by an astrologer. Hedged explicitly; I treat the gap between Lilly and Briggs's views on astrology as material.
- **"Twenty years" of computation:** hedged ("by his own reckoning, something like").
- **Second sentence of the Ussher letter:** standard, but not confirmed by a snippet in this session.
- **Briggs's journey:** the outline says he rode. I could not confirm how he traveled, so the text avoids saying.
- **Briggs's "I wonder nobody else found it out before":** paraphrased rather than quoted.

## Outline corrections

- The outline's "Briggs riding to Scotland": the mode of travel is unverified, so the text drops "riding."
- The outline's "slide rule, which put people on the Moon" overclaims. Slide rules flew as backups (Apollo 11, Apollo 13) and were used in design work. The text says just that.
- The outline's end-of-world dates are right: proposition 14 gives 1688–1700.
- The quarter-of-an-hour silence is accurate to Lilly's text. It is the source that is shaky, not the outline.
